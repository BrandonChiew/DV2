To see why a dry year hurts so much, start with where the rain falls.

Only a thin wet crescent can grow grain

Most of the continent is the colour of sand: too dry to farm. Wheat grows on the curve where the colour turns green-blue, around Perth, Adelaide, Melbourne and inland Sydney.

That crescent is a narrow margin. What happens in the years the rain doesn’t come?

El Niño is a natural swing in Pacific Ocean temperatures that usually brings dry winters to eastern Australia.

2019 had no El Niño, yet it was the driest year Australia has recorded (277.6 mm). The Bureau of Meteorology points to a very strong pattern in the Indian Ocean.

The dry columns keep landing on the dips. Is the rain really what decides it?

Farmers still sow. The rain decides the yield.

A dry year does not stop farmers planting wheat. What changes is how much each hectare gives back.

If the yield is what moves, follow it against the rain. Start with New South Wales.

In New South Wales the harvest follows the rain

Each dot is one year, joined in time order. Dry seasons sit low and to the left; wet seasons high and to the right. In 2019 NSW got about 40% of its normal rain, and its wheat yield fell to about 41% of normal.

It holds almost everywhere, except Western Australia, the biggest grower of all.

WA’s rain average is mostly desert

Western Australia’s yields barely move with its statewide rain, because the state average is mostly desert. The wheat is in the thin south-west corner, which this average hides.

Over the 37 seasons, a wetter season goes with a bigger harvest far more reliably in NSW than in WA: a correlation of 0.62 in NSW, but only 0.24 in WA.

Back in the east, one state swings harder than any other between a dry year and a wet one.

NSW swings hardest: from fourth to second

Between 2019, the driest year on record, and 2022, the record harvest, every mainland state’s wheat yield rose. New South Wales rose the most: from 0.8 to 3.0 tonnes a hectare. It went from the fourth-biggest wheat grower to the second.

Wheat isn’t the only crop in the paddock. Does everything else rise and fall with it?

The whole paddock rises and falls

Barley and canola grow in the same season, on the same farms, under the same sky. Stacked together, the drought years cut into all of them.

Put the rain, the harvest and the money side by side, and the story is the same every time.

When the rain stops, the harvest stops

In the droughts of 2002 and 2006, Australia’s wheat harvest fell by half and farmers kept about half as much money. In the wet years of 2021 and 2022 they broke records: the most money kept after costs in 2021, the biggest wheat harvest in 2022. ABARES expects a smaller crop in 2026–27, 29.9 Mt, down from 36.0 Mt. Once again, the wheat belt is watching the sky.
